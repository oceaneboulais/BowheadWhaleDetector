\begin{abstract}
% Abstracts are limited to 200 words for
% regular articles. 

Between 2007 and 2014 35 passive acoustic directional recorders were deployed along a 280 km swath of the Alaskan North Slope to detect and localize bowhead whale calls during their annual fall migration. Each season a team of manual analysts annotated the time, frequency range, distance, and type of bowhead calls, eventually labeling nearly 8.7 million call detections, one of the largest annotated bowhead whale sound datasets in existence.   A custom three-layer convolutional autoencoder compressed single-channel signal-to-noise-ratio spectrogram images into a 32-element feature vector. A simple nearest-neighbor classifier built directly on this latent space reduces the false-positive-to-true-call from 8:1 ratio to between 0.33:1 and 0.61:1, depending on the neighbor-vote threshold and minimum call duration. The same encoder trunk was used to create a convolutional neural network  classifier to distinguish calls from other transients, including seismic airgun pulses. Both variants were trained on an identical, leakage-controlled 100,000-sample dataset and evaluated on an independent, 199,825-spectrogram held-out set spanning four field seasons (2008, 2010, 2012, 2014), generalizing similarly (ROC-AUC 0.959 vs.\ 0.956, average precision 0.805 vs.\ 0.787). However, the ability to visualize the unsupervised latent space allowed rapid identification of mislabeled calls and other transients.  When the training and evaluation dataset training labels were editing, the performance of both the clustering and CNN improved by 50\%.
\end{abstract}

\section{\label{sec:1} Introduction}

The Bering-Chukchi-Beaufort (BCB) population of bowhead whales is of great cultural importance to the Alaskan natives of the North Slope Borough. In the fall, the whales’ westward migration in the Beaufort Sea supports a subsistence hunt by three villages (Utqiaġvik, Nuiqsut, and Kaktovik), and by other villages in the Chukchi Sea.  The majority of the BCB population typically summers in the eastern Beaufort Sea (e.g., \cite{MooreReeves1993}). Beginning in late August, whales travel westward along the North Slope of Alaska, heading for their overwintering grounds in the Bering Sea. The fall migration in the Beaufort Sea is generally close and parallel to shore, mostly in water depths of 20–50 m \cite{WursigClark1993}.  

Passive acoustic monitoring provides an approach for monitoring the geographic distribution, seasonality, behavioral state, and potentially the relative abundance of bowhead whales\cite{szesciorka2024basin}. During the summer and fall, bowheads produce a variety of sounds which past work has roughly divided into “simple” frequency-modulated (FM) calls and “complex” calls\cite{ljungblad1982underwater, clark1986acoustic,clark1984sounds,cummings1987sounds, moore2006listening,blackwell2007bowhead,stafford2021acoustic}.  Simple calls (Figure 1) generally include up calls, down calls, inflected calls, and undulated calls \cite{WursigClark1993}, whereas complex calls (Figure 2) include high calls, pulsed tonal and pulsive calls\cite{clark1984sounds}. The original call classification that was developed in the 1980s and 1990s is still used today, and little new research has focused on distinguishing bowhead call types during the summer and fall \cite{thode2016source,thode2017decadal,blackwell2013effects,blackwell2015effects,thode2020roaring,thode2024statistical}.

Simple sounds may serve as contact calls; for example, Ref. For complex calls, four different studies [11-13] have reported a preponderance of complex pulsive calls in active or sexually active groups of bowhead whales, but other studies [14, 15] were unable to find correlations between call types and behavior.

% ***Calls are challenging because of diversity and the presence of airguns**

The sheer volume of raw passive acoustic data from arctic waters, combined with the
need for timely analysis, has motivated the development of methods for automatically
detecting, classifying, and localizing bowhead sounds \cite{thode2012automated}. Such automated approaches have become increasingly
common across marine mammal monitoring more broadly, ranging from spectrogram-correlation
and pitch-tracking classifiers \cite{mellinger2000bowhead, baumgartner2011generalized}
to modern deep neural networks that can substantially lower false-alarm rates relative
to traditional detectors \cite{shiu2020deep, allen2021convolutional}.

However, bowhead calls are particularly challenging to detect automatically
for two reasons. First, the calls themselves are highly variable: even simple frequency-modulated sweeps so no stereotyped frequency range, duration, or spectral slope (Fig. \ref{fig:Upsweep diversity}). Furthermore, a substantial fraction of sounds are not simple frequency-modulated moans and are typically lumped into a generic "complex" category
\cite{clark1984sounds, stafford2021acoustic} that incorporates sounds variously described as growls or grunts. The second challenge to automated classification is that during the fall migration the calls
can co-occur with seismic airgun pulses that overlap the same low-frequency band
(below 500 Hz), masking true calls and generating frequency-modulated transient signals that often display similar bandwidth and time-frequency structure as bowhead calls\cite{blackwell2015effects, thode2012automated}.
%In this chapter we mine a unique long-term acoustic dataset to determine whether modern unsupervised deep learning machine learning techniques can be used to improve the detection and classification of bowhead whales sounds during their fall migrations.  

A previous automated detector and localizer has previously been developed by one of the PIs, that could detect specific call types but not classify them \cite{thode2012automated}.  This original algorithm used image processing to create hand-crafted feature vectors that were then passed through a simple three-layer neural network to flag whether a sound was a whale call or not.  This algorithm could thus be used to compute call rates and call spatial densities and has been applied to multiple previous studies\cite{thode2016source,thode2017decadal,thode2020roaring,thode2024statistical,blackwell2015effects}.  


Here we apply modern supervised convolutional neural networks (CNN) and unsupervised convolutional autoencoders (CAE) \cite{guo2017deep,ozanich2021deep} to extract latent feature vectors directly from spectrogram images, without human design and input, to build an updated bowhead whale call detector.  We find that both approaches perform similarly; however, the unsupervised approach provides a convenient way of visualizing the structure of the dataset, permitting rapid correction of mislabeled data samples.  When these edited samples are used to train the algorithms, the detection performance increases substantially.


%We then evaluate whether initializing a supervised call and non-call classifier from this unsupervised representation (rather than training the same classifier architecture from random weights) measurably improves detection performance.

Section II describes the equipment, deployment geometry, recordings, data preprocessing, and initial manual analysis used to create both training and evaluation datasets.  It then describes the convolutional autoencoder architecture used to process the data, the supervised classifier built on top of it, and the training and evaluation procedures used to compare the two initialization strategies.  Section III reports the resulting detection performance of both unsupervised and supervised approached using the evaluation datasets.  The Discussion in Section IV highlights the importance of identifying and removing data samples that contain multiple sources, such as two whale calls, or a whale call and an airgun.  It also discusses the utility of using dimensionality reduction and visualization methods on the latent feature space to correct and re-label mis-classified data, substantially improving detector performance.

\subsection{\label{sec:DataOverview}Acoustic Data}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/DASAR_map.jpg}
    \caption{:  Locations of the five DASAR sites (arrays, sites 1-5) in the Beaufort Sea, 2007–2014.  The inset shows the location of the map on the north coast of Alaska.  After 2011 Site 4 “flipped” four DASAR locations from the blue triangles to the hollow orange triangles.  This chapter uses data from Sites 3 and 5.}
    \label{fig:DASAR_map}

Between 2007 and 2014, as part of their exploration activities in the Beaufort Sea, the Shell Exploration and Production Company implemented an acoustic monitoring program to study the effects of industrial activities, particularly seismic airgun surveys, on bowhead whales during their fall migration (August–October). For roughly 90 days arrays of Directional Autonomous Seafloor Acoustic Recorders (DASARs)\citep{greene2004directional} were deployed at five sites in the Beaufort Sea between Kaktovik and Harrison Bay, a 280 km span (Fig. \ref{fig:DASAR_map})\cite{blackwell2015effects}.  

DASARs are autonomous recording packages equipped with an DIFAR sonobuoy vector sensor package\cite{holler2014evolution,Kuzu6459697_DIFAR}, consisting of a omnidirectional acoustic pressure sensor (sensitivity of 149 dB re 1 $\mu$Pa/V) and two horizontal directional sensors capable of measuring the north-south and east-west components of acoustic particle velocity\cite{greene2004directional}.  This arrangement permits the azimuth of received sounds, such as bowhead whale calls, to be measured from individual DASARs.  Each time series is sampled at 1 kHz with a maximum usable acoustic frequency of 450 Hz due to antialiasing filter roll-off.  
Each site consisted of three to 11 DASARs deployed in a triangular grid with 7 km separation; individual instruments are labeled ‘A’ through ‘G’ from south to north (Fig. 3).  

% Within a site, a unique call is often detected across multiple DASARS, and the resulting bearings can be triangulated to measure a whale’s location.  To avoid confusion, we define a “unique call” (UC) as a sound generated by a whale at a specific time and location, while a “call detection” (CD) is defined as the signal detected by a specific DASAR resulting from that unique call.  Thus, multiple CDs can result from a single UC, and by triangulating the bearings from the set of CDs (“CD set”) the generation time and location of the original UC can be estimated.  As a byproduct the tracking system records samples of the same UC at multiple ranges; however, the positions and ranges of calls are not used in the deep learning analysis presented here.

\subsection{Initial Manual Review}

During each deployment season,  a subset of data (typically 5 – 8 days) was also reviewed by experienced manual analysts with multiple years of experience. An analyst would simultaneously view spectrograms from multiple DASARs at a site, with each image displaying the same 60 s interval. 

Over the span of eight years, various trained analysts generated 8,690,929 manually annotated whale calls (MAWCs) from all sites and deployments, along with each signal's frequency span, duration, and bearing.  By viewing multiple spectrograms simultaneously, the analysts were able to link sets of CDs to the same UC, and triangulate the UC location.  The ability to review multiple DASARs also allowed the analysts to detect weaker call detections on DASARs that might have been overlooked had the spectrograms been reviewed individually.  


Analysts also classified the UC into various types of simple and complex calls, including up calls, down calls, undulations, tonal (constant frequency), and “complex calls”, a catch-all category that spans various kinds of calls that cannot be assigned to another category.  This "original manual" database (OMD) remains one of the largest, if not the largest, compiled datasets of bowhead sounds in existence. However, calls were rarely reviewed by more than one analyst, and the specific members of the analyst team changed across the years of the project, so differences in performance between analysts were never quantified or quality-checked.  Furthermore, by reviewing 60 s of data at a time on a monitor sharing multiple spectrograms, reviewers did occasionally miss shorter, weaker calls.

\subsection{Training and evaluation datasets}
\subsubsection{Master database: Dates and locations}
Both the training and evaluation datasets were sampled from a master database (MD) that contained a mixture of call detections (CD) and other transients (OT).  All signals were obtained from a subset of years, dates, and locations in the OMD, specifically DASARS ‘A’,’D’, and ‘G’ (representing the shallowest, median, and deepest water depths at a site) at both Sites 3 and 5 during the years 2008, 2010, 2012, and 2014, yielding a total of 24 year/Site/DASAR combinations.  This spread in locations and times ensured that the deep learning algorithms would be trained on managable sample size that covered both a variety of water depths (between 30 and 55 m) and  the full time span of the project.  The latter factor is an important consideration, as evidence exists that the frequency content of the bowhead calls evolved across the years of the experiment\cite{thode2017decadal}.

For a given year, the three DASARs at both sites (six total) were sampled on the same day, but the dates sampled varied between years, as the DASARs were deployed on slightly different dates each year.  Any difference between days sampled at Sites 3 and 5 for a given year arose because the sites were deployed and recovered at slightly different dates at the start and end of that year’s deployment. \subsubsection{\label{sec:TransientDetector}Master database: extracting whale calls and other transients}

The Beaufort sea data contains transient sounds other than bowhead whale calls, including random fluctuations from wind-driven noise, non-acoustic bangs and striations arising from currents, other biologic sounds, vessel and drilling noises, and seismic airgun surveys.  

Of particular concern were seismic airgun sounds\cite{dragoset2000introduction,guerra2011quantifying}, which are generated by arrays of seismic airguns as part of surveys for oil and gas in the seafloor.  These sounds are produced at regular intervals ranging from 8 to 40 seconds, depending on the water depth beneath the surveying vessel.  The evaluation of the impact of these sounds during oil and gas exploration in the Beaufort over this time period was the original motivation and focus of these deployments. \cite{blackwell2015effects,blackwell2017effects}

The primary energy of these airguns occurs over the same bandwidth as bowhead whale calls, and as these low-frequency signals propagate through this shallow-water environment, the signals take on the characteristics of frequency-modulated downsweeps that mimic the structure of bowhead downswept calls.  Thus the development of a bowhead call detector in this environment required an ability to discriminate calls from airgun signals.  Hence, the inclusion of a wide variety of transient signals that are not whale calls ("other transients", or OT), is essential when building the training and evaluation datasets.

To extract transient signals from the raw acoustic data streams, a simple transient detector was applied to DASARs A,D, and G over the full 24-hour dataset for each day and Site listed in Table \ref{tab:training_dates}.  No information from the OMD was applied during this initial event detection stage, to ensure that whale calls would be processed consistently with all other transient signals.

The transient detector was an “energy” constant false alarm rate (CFAR) transient detector originally developed for the original 2010 detection algorithm\cite{thode2012automated}.  The detector uses a 256-point Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) with 75\% overlap to compute a moving average of the background noise spectrum to which a new incoming spectrum was compared.  The time constant (equalization time) of the moving average was 25 seconds, and the spectrogram was converted to units of power spectral density (dB re 1 $\mu Pa^2$/Hz).   A filter bank of detectors was created, each covering 40 Hz in bandwidth, overlapped 50\%.  The lowest frequency detector thus spanned 25 to 65 Hz, the second band 45 to 85 Hz, and so on up to 450 Hz. The purpose of running a filter bank of detectors was to avoid missing signals that were locally narrow band but high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).  Other transient detectors are also available for enhancing detection of locally narrow band signals across a wide frequency span \cite{wang2001all, helble2012generalized}.

The start of a new detection was logged whenever the integrated spectrum of any detection band in a new incoming FFT exceeded 5 dB SNR relative to the corresponding background noise band, and that threshold endured for at least 100 msec.  The detection was logged as ending when all detection bands fell below the 5 dB threshold.  Detections logged less than 50 msec from the previous detection were merged with the previous detection.  

Once detected, a new transient's start and end time was compared against every MAWC in the OMD to see whether the transient's time window overlapped a manual annotation's time window by 50\% or more (ignoring annotations flagged as seals or unknown biologics).  If this overlap threshold was exceeded, the transient was flagged as a call detection (CD) and assigned the classification label associated with the annotation.  Otherwise the transient was flagged as an "other transient" (OT).  If a transient was sufficiently long to encompass multiple annotations, the earliest annotation was used.

The collection of OTs obtained from a single date/DASAR combination was run through a simple interval detector\cite{guerra2011quantifying} to flag whether the OT was part of a sequence of airgun pulses.  This information was not incorporated into any deep-learning architecture, but was used to estimate the fraction of airgun signals present in the OT, and help label the presence of airguns in the unsupervised learning results.

For every transient (regardless of whether it is matched with OMD), five seconds of acoustic data were extracted from the omnidirectional channel, centered around the time of maximum peak power encountered across any filterbank.  Centering the time window around the peak power point turned out to be a crucial step in the detector performance, because deep learning networks (even convolutional ones) are sensitive to the relative translational position of an object in an image.  

For each time series a new spectrogram was created with a 256 pt FFT (90\% overlap) and converted to units of power spectral density (dB re 1 $\mu Pa^2$/Hz).  The median background spectrum was estimated from samples present in the first and last seconds of the time window and then subtracted from the middle three seconds of the spectrogram, to convert the image into units of dB SNR.  The final spectrogram image thus spanned a fixed time window of three seconds and a bandwidth of 25 to 500 Hz, translating into 128 frequency bins by 119 time bins.  The images were stored as unsigned 8-bit integer resolution to make efficient use of memory.

An important consequence of this processing is that a nontrivial number of spectrograms, both CD and OT, could contain multiple signals from multiple sources, as the transient detector merges detections that overlap in time and/or frequency.  The presence of multiple signals became a key consideration when training and evaluating the deep learning detectors.

A total of 2,116,248 transient signals (238,751 CD, 1,877,497 OT) were extracted from the dates and locations shown in Table \ref{tab:training_dates}, a ratio of 7.86 OTs  per CD.  However, this ratio, when computed over one day, varied substantially by date and Site.  This collection of signals, dubbed the "master database" (MD), provided the samples for the distinct training and evaluation datasets.

\subsubsection{Construction of training and evaluation datasets}
\label{sec:ConstructionTrainEval}

From the master database both a 100,000 sample 'training' dataset and a 200,000 sample 'evaluation' dataset were constructed.

For the training dataset, we targeted a 50/50 split in CD and OTs without replacement from the master database, to create a more balanced dataset with greater representation of whale calls.  Both CDs and OTs were sampled in proportion to their total distribution across year, day, site, and DASAR, such that the dataset accurately reproduced the relative presence of both signal types across time and space.  If certain combinations of dates and DASAR had fewer CDs than the number requested by the database (i.e. the size of the training database is very large), then sampling with replacement was allowed for that particular combination of date and DASAR.  Only 2 out of the $\approx$125 combinations of dates and DASARs required sampling with replacement.  Because of this proportionally restriction, the final raw training dataset actually consisted of 49,566 CDs and 50,434 OTs, with 40.4\% of the OTs being seismic airgun signals (20,365 out of 50,434).
  

For the evaluation dataset, we targeted an 8:1 ratio of OT to CD, to mimic the expected relative occurrence of these signal types whenever a simple transient detector was applied to this dataset.  Selecting an appropriate value of this ratio is important whenever computing the 'precision' or 'false discovery rate' metrics, which are affected by the number of true negatives in the dataset.  The evaluation database was sampled from the master database in the same exact manner as the training database, except that if a random sample initially selected for the evaluation database also occurred in the training database, it was rejected and replaced with a new random sample.  Thus no overlapping samples existed between the training and evaluation datasets.  One consequence of this procedure is that date/DASAR combinations with very low numbers of CDs may not be present in the final evaluation dataset, which consisted of 177,666 OTs and 22,159 CDs, with 40.5\% of the OTs being seismic airgun signals (71,981).

For every spectrogram sample, the time and frequency at which the peak signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was documented, along with an estimate of the duration of the signal obtained by the transient detector described in the previous section.  These extracted features will be used in Sec. to interpret the organization of the clustering map produced by the deep learning autoencoder.

Because a single unique call (UC) is frequently detected redundantly across multiple DASARs (A/D/G) on the same recording day, a naive random
per-image train/test split could potentially place near-duplicate views of the same
call on both sides of the split, inflating performance metrics. All
train/validation/test splits were therefore constructed using a
\emph{grouped}, stratified partitioning scheme, with the group key set to
the date$\times$site combination (51 distinct date$\times$site groups
across the full 100,000-image training draw): every image recorded at a
given site on a given day was assigned entirely to one partition (train,
validation, or test), and the partitions were verified computationally to
share zero group overlap. Models were trained on a nominal 70/15/15
train/validation/test split (realized here as 70,870 training images
across 36 groups, 12,746 validation images across 7 groups, and 16,384
test images across 8 groups, each partition retaining an approximately
balanced call fraction of 0.49--0.53), with the validation split used only
for early-stopping model selection.

while ROC-AUC (a prevalence-invariant metric) was computed on the full
held-out set as-is.

The unsupervised deep learning architecture used for this study was a convolutional autoencoder \cite{hinton2006reducing}, which has been successful in the past for compressing acoustic signals \cite{ozanich2021deep,chien2023automatic}. We built the system in the open-source framework PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch}, which began by normalizing each training spectrogram image (121 frequency bins $\times$ 104 time bins) so its pixel values ranged from 0 to 1 using min-max normalization applied independently to each sample. The image was then passed through three successive convolutional blocks, each consisting of a 3$\times$3 convolutional kernel with stride one and same-padding (padding=1), followed by batch normalization \cite{ioffe2015batch}, a ReLU activation \cite{nair2010rectified}, and 2$\times$2 max pooling \cite{lecun1998gradient}. The number of feature channels increased from 32 to 64 to 128 across the three blocks, while max pooling halved both spatial dimensions at each step, reducing the 121$\times$104 input to 15$\times$13 feature maps after the third block. The resulting 128-channel feature maps were flattened to a 24,960-element vector (128 $\times$ 15 $\times$ 13) and passed through two fully connected layers (24,960$\to$64$\to$32) with a ReLU activation between them, producing a 32-dimensional latent vector. The decoder first expands this latent vector back through two fully connected layers (32$\to$64$\to$24,960) with ReLU, reshapes the result to 128$\times$15$\times$13, and then applies three transposed convolutional layers (2$\times$2 kernel, stride 2) that progressively expand the feature maps from 128 to 64 to 32 channels — the first two blocks followed by batch normalization and ReLU — before a final transposed convolution projects back to a single-channel output at the original 121$\times$104 resolution. The decoder output is not passed through a sigmoid or clamping function, leaving the reconstruction unconstrained in value. Training was performed using the Adam optimizer \cite{kingma2015adam} (learning rate of $10^-3$) to minimize pixel-wise mean squared error (MSE) between the input and reconstructed spectrogram. Figure \ref{fig:autoencoder_architecture} illustrates the full encoder–decoder structure.

The system was trained for 100 epochs on the training dataset with a mini-batch size of 32. Reconstructed images were spot-checked against original images to qualitatively verify that the latent space had sufficient dimensions to reproduce the original signals (Fig.~\ref{fig:Reconstruction_Example}). Through this approach we determined that 16 dimensions were insufficient for representing the full repertoire of whale calls. 

To use the AE latent space as a detector, a nearest-neighbor algorithm (Euclidean distance) was used to compare a given evaluation sample against the samples in the training dataset.  The fraction of the 40 nearest training neighbors that were labeled as CDs was then compared against a threshold to determine whether the evaluation sample would be classified as a whale call.  By adjusting the threshold a complete precision/recall curve could be generated.  The performance of the detector varied little whether 10 to 80 neighbors were used.

To construct the supervised detector, the autoencoder decoder was removed
and the encoder was retained as a feature-extraction backbone. The encoder
mapped each input spectrogram through three
convolution--batch-normalization--ReLU--max-pooling blocks, followed by
fully connected layers of 64 and 32 units, to produce a 32-dimensional
feature vector (Fig.~\ref{fig:autoencoder_architecture}). A newly added
fully connected classification layer mapped this feature vector to two
logits, corresponding to call detection (CD) and other transient (OT).
The network was trained end-to-end using class-weighted cross-entropy,
with CDs treated as the positive class and OTs as the negative class.
Softmax normalization was applied to the two logits only when class
probabilities were required for evaluation.

Both variants were trained with the Adam optimizer (learning rate
$10^{-3}$, batch size 32) on the grouped 70/15/15 train/validation/test
split described below (Sec.~\ref{sec:CNNRelabel... or splits section}),
stopping after 10 epochs without improvement in validation ROC-AUC
(defined in Table~\ref{tab:cnn_results}). Both approaches stopped at
epoch 12, with the best validation ROC-AUC obtained at epoch 2 in both
cases -- 0.9533 for the scratch model and 0.9469 for the warm-start
model -- after which validation ROC-AUC declined monotonically while
training loss continued to fall, indicating overfitting to the training
partition beyond this point; the reported models throughout this paper
are the epoch-2 checkpoints, not the epoch-12 (overfit) weights. We note
that checkpoint selection used ROC-AUC rather than average precision;
because ROC-AUC is prevalence-invariant it can be less sensitive to
detection quality than AP under our test-time realistic prevalence
($\approx$1:9), and AP-based early stopping is a preferable criterion we
plan to adopt in a future retraining pass.

In practice, little difference was noted between the scratch and warm-start model's performance, so for the rest of the paper we will use the **scratch(?)-trained** results.




\subsection{Dataset dimensionality reduction,  visualization, and final editing}
\label{sec:Dataset_Dimen_Vis_Editing}

Both the autoencoder and custom CNN provide 32-dimensional latent space representations of spectrograms.  As will be discussed in more detail shortly, visualizing the latent space structure of the signal datasets provided powerful capabilities for flagging and correcting mislabeled data, as well as interpreting how different types of signals map into the abstract space.  For example, this latter ability alerted us to the particular challenges accompanying spectrograms with multiple signals. 

Visualization of the datasets were accomplished by transforming 32-dimensional latent vectors into an analogous three-dimensional coordinate system using the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm \cite{mcinnes2018umap} with 15 nearest neighbors and a minimum distance of 0.1.  A special graphical user interface (GUI) was written in the programming language MATLAB that permitted an analyst to display and rotate the three-dimensional latent space UMAP representation, and then color the samples according to whether they were assigned as CD or OT(Section \ref{sec:ConstructionTrainEval}).  The label coloring could also be altered to show the peak frequency, time of peak frequency, transient duration, call type assigned by a manual analyst, or whether the signal arose from an airgun shot. A user could then select a subset of samples and review their associated input images. 

Figure \ref{fig:UMP displays} displays three views of the 3-D representation of the initial training and evaluation datasets, taken by rotating the display around one of the UMAP coordinate axes.  Each point represents a single spectrogram, with red points representing CDs, and blue points OTs.  The points in the space have been translated to be centered on the dataset mean and scaled with respect to the standard deviation of each axes.  Points close to each other in the abstract 3-D space represent spectrograms with similar features, while points widely separated indicate spectrograms with widely different features.

The dense arch-like zone of red points suggests that the unsupervised autoencoder is having some success in clustering CD whale calls from other signals; however, one notes regions where red and blue points are mixed together.  A preliminary review of the spectrograms from those regions found several reasons for this blend:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{False CDs}: Some CD spectrograms were not whale calls, typically misclassified weak airgun signals;
    \item \textit{Multiple signals}: A CD or OT spectrogram did contain a whale call, but also contained a second signal, such as a second whale call, an airgun, or other distinct transient;
    \item \textit{Missed CD}: An OT spectrogram incorporated a legitimate whale call not contained in the OMD;
    \item \textit{Valid clustering}: Whale calls and airgun signals were legitimately similar to each other;
\end{enumerate}

In particular, the presence of multiple signals often created a latent space vector significantly different from vectors with only single whale calls, even in situations where both signals were distinct whale calls themselves.

To evaluate the impact of these factors on deep learning detectors, we modified the visualization GUI to permit three co-authors to review and re-label samples as either true CD, OT, or a new "multiple signals" category. Beyond serving as a feature-extraction backbone for the supervised classifier
(Sec.~\ref{sec:cnn_classifier}), the autoencoder's 32-dimensional latent space
proved useful as a diagnostic tool for auditing the training dataset itself.
Because every spectrogram in the 100,000-image training pool has a
corresponding point in this compressed representation, the full dataset
could be visualized as a low-dimensional UMAP projection
(Sec.~\ref{sec:autoencoder}) and inspected by neighborhood rather than by
exhaustively re-listening to or re-viewing spectrograms in collection order.
This was the practical mechanism behind the dataset-editing pass described
above (Sec.~\ref{sec:Dataset_Dimen_Vis_Editing}): images containing multiple
overlapping signals, whale calls that had not been logged in the manual
database, and transients that had been mislabeled as calls (most often
airgun pulses masking a genuine call) were located by identifying anomalous
or mixed-label clusters in the latent-space projection and then auditing
the comparatively small number of spectrograms responsible for them, rather
than by reviewing the entire corpus.

In effect, the latent space turns the problem of finding labeling errors
scattered across tens of thousands of automatically-compiled images into
the much smaller problem of explaining a handful of outlying or mixed
clusters --- an approach whose value should only grow as
automatically-labeled bioacoustic datasets reach a scale (Sec.~\ref{sec:1})
that precludes full manual re-verification. Both the original annotations
and the revised annotations were stored with the datasets and used to
evaluate to what extent misclassified and multiple-source data impacts the
deep learning performance. From this point on, the \emph{original dataset}
is defined as the training or evaluation dataset as originally generated by
the procedures in Sec.~\ref{sec:ConstructionTrainEval}, while the
\emph{revised dataset} is defined as the same samples after final review by
co-authors in the UMAP space (Sec.~\ref{sec:Dataset_Dimen_Vis_Editing}).

We note that this account of the latent space's diagnostic value is
qualitative: we did not separately track how many labeling corrections were
made through this route versus other QA steps, nor compare it against
alternative auditing strategies (e.g., ranking by classifier confidence or
reconstruction error alone); quantifying the efficiency of
latent-space-guided auditing relative to these alternatives would be a
natural follow-up analysis.

\subsection{Evaluation details}

All networks were trained on both the original and revised training data.  Networks trained on the original data where then evaluated using the original evaluation datasets, and those trained on revised training data were evaluated with the revised evaluation data.

For networks using the revised datasets, spectrograms classified as "multiple signals" or "unknown signal" were treated as OT.  PR-AUC (precision-recall area-under-curve) and ROC-AUC **ref** was calculated.  The PR-AUC is the preferred metric for unbalanced datasets; although the evaluation dataset is not extremely unbalanced (~8:1 ratio), using the PR-AUC seems a wise choice.

Evaluations were also performed used signals restricted to durations of 0.5 second or longer.  This duration threshold was chosen based on feedback from manual analysts, who felt that whale calls shorter than 0.5 s were often difficult to identify as whale calls.

\subsection{Effect of dataset relabeling on CNN detection performance}
\label{sec:CNNRelabel}

The dataset-editing pass described in Sec.~\ref{sec:DatasetEditing} raises a
natural question: how much of the scratch CNN's detection performance
(Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_results}) is attributable to the classifier itself,
versus the quality of the labels it is scored against? To isolate this, the
same frozen scratch-CNN checkpoint used in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_results}
was scored exactly once on the 199,825-spectrogram independent evaluation
set, producing a single, fixed set of call probabilities; these same
probabilities were then re-graded against two label sets pulled from the
same underlying MATLAB export: the \emph{original}, pre-review labels
(\texttt{type\_org}) and the manually \emph{reviewed} labels (\texttt{iscall})
produced by the latent-space-guided relabeling pass of
Sec.~\ref{sec:DatasetEditing}. Because the model's outputs are identical in
both cases, any difference in reported performance isolates the effect of
correcting mislabeled evaluation examples from any change in the detector
itself. In total, 13,528 of the 199,825 labels (6.8\%) were changed by the
review, most often airgun pulses previously mislabeled as calls, or calls
missed by the original manual pass.

**Relabeling changes ROC-AUC and average precision in opposite directions
(Sec.~\ref{sec:CNNRelabel} background above): ROC-AUC improves slightly from
0.964 to 0.965 ($\Delta$ROC-AUC=+0.002), while average precision (computed
over the full curve, at each label set's own native prevalence) falls
substantially from 0.809 to 0.722 ($\Delta$AP=$-$0.087). Because ROC-AUC and
AP are each sensitive to prevalence and curve-averaging in different ways, we
also compare the two label sets at a single, matched operating point --
miss fraction fixed at 10\% for both -- which isolates the review's effect
on detection quality from any prevalence-driven artifact
(Table~\ref{tab:cnn_relabel_results}, Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_relabel_pr_fdr}): at
this matched recall level, FDR falls from 0.448 (original labels) to 0.426
(reviewed labels), a smaller but still genuine, prevalence-controlled
improvement.** This is consistent with the review's main effect being a
reduction in call prevalence, from 0.1109 to 0.1011 (labels removed from the
call class outnumbering labels added to it, though by a narrower margin than
an earlier, smaller review pass): ROC-AUC is prevalence-invariant and
responds only to how well the ranking separates the two classes, and that
separation improves once mislabeled examples (chiefly airgun pulses
previously counted as calls) are removed from the positive class. **Average
precision, in contrast, is sensitive to class prevalence, so the same
reduction in positive-class size mechanically lowers the achievable AP even
though the underlying ranking is measurably better, and the matched-operating-point FDR comparison confirms that this is exactly what happens: performance at a fixed recall genuinely improves after review, rather than merely appearing to improve or worsen depending on which prevalence-sensitive summary statistic is reported.** Taken together, these
results indicate that the classifier's ranking ability was, if anything,
mildly \emph{understated} by the original, noisier labels, and that the
latent-space-guided relabeling pass of Sec.~\ref{sec:DatasetEditing} is
recovering a more accurate, rather than more favorable, picture of scratch
CNN detection performance -- though the magnitude of the AP drop at native
prevalence is large enough that AP alone should not be read as the review's
headline effect; the matched-recall FDR comparison is the more reliable
summary of the review's net impact on detection quality.

\subsection{The impact of multiple signals and mislabeled data on detector performance}

A result that at first appears counter-intuitive is reported in
Sec.~\ref{sec:CNNRelabel}: scoring the \emph{same}, frozen scratch-CNN
predictions against the manually reviewed (post-review) labels yields a
\emph{lower} average precision (0.722) than scoring those identical
predictions against the original, noisier labels (0.809), even though the
review is, by construction, a strict improvement in label quality (13,528 of
199,825 labels, 6.8\%, were corrected, predominantly airgun pulses
previously mislabeled as calls). This is not evidence that the review
degraded ground truth, nor that the classifier's ranking of examples became
worse; rather, it is an expected consequence of how average precision (AP)
and ROC-AUC behave differently under a change in the evaluation set's class
prevalence.

ROC-AUC is, by construction, invariant to class prevalence: it summarizes
how well a classifier separates positives from negatives purely in terms of
true- and false-positive \emph{rates}, which are each normalized by the
number of positives and negatives respectively and are therefore unaffected
by the ratio between the two class sizes \cite{fawcett2006introduction}.
Average precision, by contrast, is computed from precision, which depends
directly on the absolute number of false positives relative to true
positives at a given recall, and is therefore mechanically sensitive to the
prevalence of the positive class: at any fixed true- and false-positive
rate, a rarer positive class yields a lower precision purely because there
are more negatives available to generate false positives
\cite{davis2006relationship,saito2015precision,he2009learning}. The review
reduced the prevalence of the call class from 11.09\% (original labels) to
10.11\% (reviewed labels) -- a $\sim$9\% relative reduction in positive
examples, driven mostly by the removal of airgun pulses previously
mislabeled as calls. Because AP at a given ranking quality scales
approximately with positive-class prevalence, while ROC-AUC does not, this
prevalence shift alone is sufficient to produce the AP decrease
(0.809$\to$0.722) and the slight ROC-AUC \emph{increase}
(0.964$\to$0.965) we observe, without implying that the classifier's
underlying discrimination ability became worse (Sec.~\ref{sec:CNNRelabel}).
If anything, the small ROC-AUC improvement after review suggests the
opposite: once labeling noise, chiefly airgun pulses that had been
incorrectly logged as calls in the original manual annotations, is removed
from the positive class, the ranking separation between true calls and
non-calls becomes marginally \emph{cleaner}, consistent with the broader
literature on the detrimental effect of label noise on both training and
evaluation of supervised classifiers \cite{frenay2014classification,
northcutt2021confident}.

The matched-operating-point comparison in Table~\ref{tab:cnn_relabel_results}
(false discovery rate at a fixed 10\% miss fraction, i.e.\ recall=0.90) is
necessary precisely because AP and ROC-AUC each confound genuine detection
quality with a prevalence artifact in different ways, and so neither curve-
averaged scalar alone can disambiguate ``the review changed the data'' from
``the review changed the classifier's apparent competence.'' By fixing
recall and reading off the corresponding false discovery rate directly from
the same precision-recall curve for each label set (rather than averaging
precision over all recall levels, as AP does, or treating true/false
positive rates symmetrically as ROC-AUC does), this comparison holds the
operating condition fixed and isolates the net effect of relabeling on
detection quality at that condition: FDR falls modestly, from 0.448
(original labels) to 0.426 (reviewed labels), at the matched 10\%-miss
operating point. This confirms that once prevalence is controlled for, the
review's net effect is a genuine, if modest, improvement in detection
quality rather than an artifact of the summary statistic chosen -- consistent
with prior observations that precision-recall-based summaries, including AP,
should be interpreted cautiously, and compared at matched operating points
or prevalence-normalized forms, when the class balance of an evaluation set
changes between conditions \cite{davis2006relationship,saito2015precision,
he2009learning}.

\item How the detector distinguishes airgun signals from whale calls.
    \item How points associated with single whale calls are sorted by duration, peak frequency, and FM slope.
    \item The sensitivity of the convolutional networks to the timing of the signal within the spectrogram, and why this fact maps multiple-signal spectrograms into distinctive coordinates of the feature space.

\subsubsection{The surprising ability to distinguish airgun signals from whale calls without interval context}

The ability for the latent space to cleanly separate the major of seismic airgun signals from whale calls was surprising, given the great diversity in time frequency structure of airguns, and the fact that the detector did not have access to contextual information like whether similar signals were occurring at regular intervals.  The backwards J-shape and vertical component of many airgun signals seems sufficient distinctive to distinguish from most bowhead whale downsweeps.

The UMAP projection also shows trajectories of points generated by individual seismic surveys.  As a survey changes distance from a DASAR, the time-frequency structure of the signal gradually changes due to waveguide propagation effects, resulting in a smooth continuous evolution of the feature vectors associated with the airgun signals.

Future work should consider adding a simple interval detector for crude context input into the detector.

\subsubsection{How timing of signals within spectrogram allows identification of multiple-signal situations}
A design choice with implications for both the autoencoder and the
downstream classifier is the fixed-duration, peak-power-centered spectrogram
window described in Sec.~\ref{sec:TransientDetector}: rather than aligning each
image to, for example, the detector's onset time, every spectrogram is
centered on the time of maximum local power spectral density encountered
between 25 and 450 Hz. This choice was made specifically to reduce the
tendency of the autoencoder to encode a signal's absolute temporal position
within the window as a latent feature, since a purely reconstructive
objective has no explicit incentive toward time-shift invariance and would
otherwise be free to map same-call, different-alignment spectrograms to
different, disconnected regions of the latent space.

We have not, however, performed a controlled ablation that directly
quantifies how sensitive either the latent representation or the downstream
classifier's accuracy is to this alignment convention — for example, by
re-centering the same held-out calls at several offsets from the peak-power
point and measuring the resulting change in embedding distance or classifier
score. The consistency of classifier performance across four field seasons,
two DASAR sites, and three DASAR channels (Sec.~\ref{sec:Results}) is
reassuring but only an indirect argument, since it does not rule out the
alignment convention behaving differently for calls of different type,
duration, or SNR than it does in aggregate. We therefore flag this
explicitly as an open question rather than a validated property of the
pipeline: a direct sensitivity analysis along these lines would clarify
whether peak-power centering is a genuine robustness safeguard, a
performance-neutral convention, or a source of latent hidden bias inherited
from the original detection pipeline (Sec.~\ref{sec:TransientDetector}).

\section{\label{sec:Conclusion}Conclusion}

We built and evaluated a two-stage deep learning pipeline — an unsupervised
convolutional autoencoder followed by a supervised convolutional classifier
sharing its encoder architecture — for distinguishing bowhead whale calls
from other transient sounds, including seismic airgun pulses, in passive
acoustic recordings from the Alaskan North Slope. Both a randomly-initialized
classifier and a classifier warm-started from the autoencoder's encoder
weights were trained on an identical, leakage-controlled 100,000-sample
dataset and evaluated, without further tuning, on a fully independent
held-out set of 199,825 spectrograms spanning four field seasons (2008,
2010, 2012, 2014), two DASAR sites, and three DASAR channels
(Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_results}). Both classifiers generalized well and
similarly to this unseen data (ROC-AUC of 0.959 and 0.956, average precision
of 0.805 and 0.787, for the scratch and warm-start models respectively), and
this small performance gap was stable across every year/site/DASAR subgroup
examined (Sec.~\ref{sec:Results}), indicating a genuine, if modest, property
of the two initialization strategies on this task rather than an artifact of
a particular subset of the data. Contrary to our initial expectation,
unsupervised autoencoder pretraining did not confer a measurable
generalization advantage over training the same architecture from random
initialization on this dataset. **A simpler nearest-neighbor detector built
directly on the autoencoder's 32-dimensional latent space, requiring no
supervised training at all, reduced the false-positive-to-true-call ratio
from the 2010 detector's natural, unfiltered baseline of approximately 8:1
to between 0.33:1 and 0.61:1 depending on label set and duration filter
(Sec.~\ref{sec:AEkNN}, Table~\ref{tab:ae_knn_results}); because it depends on
only a single hyperparameter, this AE+kNN result is currently our most
readily reproducible finding and the one we recommend as the primary point
of comparison against the original 2010 detector.**

Two aspects of the pipeline merit further attention, as discussed above.
First, the autoencoder's latent space proved to be a practical tool for
auditing the training dataset for labeling errors (Sec.~\ref{sec:DatasetEditing}),
suggesting that latent-space-guided review could be formalized into a
reusable quality-assurance step for future automatically-compiled bioacoustic
datasets, rather than remaining an ad hoc part of dataset construction.
Second, while the spectrogram images are deliberately centered on peak local
power to reduce time-shift sensitivity in the latent space, we have not
directly measured how sensitive detection performance or the latent
representation itself is to this alignment convention, and we flag this as a
concrete direction for follow-up analysis. More broadly, this detector
operates at the coarse level of distinguishing a call from a non-call
transient; the seven manually-annotated call sub-types (Types 1--7) present
in the training data were not modeled here, and discriminating among them
remains an open target for future work building on the representation
developed in this study.
